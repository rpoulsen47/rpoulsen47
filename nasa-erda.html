North Central Colorado Wildland Fires

Evaluating aspen response to wildfire and drought in Colorado to understand changes in moose habitat.

Overview

Quaking aspen provide important habitat for many species, including Shiras moose. In recent decades, aspen distributions have shifted due to wildfires and drought. Colorado Parks and Wildlife is interested in quantifying changes in aspen forest due to disturbance to understand the implications for moose carrying capacity. To address these goals, we mapped areas of aspen expansion and contraction in Northern Colorado, where severe wildfires burned in 2020, and areas of forest decline in Grand Mesa, which has experienced long-term drought. We used these areas to investigate the environmental factors associated with aspen mortality and recovery.

Abstract

Across the western United States, quaking aspen is an ecologically significant tree species, providing habitat for a variety of animals. In Colorado, Shiras moose rely on aspen for habitat and browse throughout their summer and winter ranges. However, disturbances such as drought and wildfires have influenced aspen extent change, posing questions about moose carrying capacity and forest management practices throughout the state. In partnership with Colorado Parks and Wildlife (CPW), this project aimed to harness NASA Earth observations and remote sensing techniques to examine aspen distribution following the 2020 Cameron Peak and East Troublesome fires in Northern Colorado. To do this, we produced an aspen change map depicting the differences in distribution between 2019 and 2025 and identified the factors associated with aspen extent change. Through this research, we found elevation, burn severity, and slope to be strong predictors of aspen change after the fires. We also applied LandTrendr, a remote sensing image analysis algorithm, to characterize the timing and magnitude of aspen decline in Grand Mesa, Colorado, an area experiencing long-term drought. The results from this project can provide insight on how moose habitat may change in the future and will be used by CPW to inform their 2027 field campaign to estimate forage availability in Colorado's aspen forests.

Benefit to Partner

Colorado Parks and Wildlife has an ongoing study to estimate Shiras moose habitat and forage availability in Colorado. The maps of aspen change and decline produced by this project directly support this study. These maps identify the locations of aspen extent change in Northern Colorado and in the Grand Mesa, which inform CPW's field campaign for summer 2027, to estimate forage availability in Colorado's aspen forests. Additionally, this project provides a high-level understanding of the trends and associated environmental factors in aspen extent change in Colorado after the 2020 wildfires and long-term drought, which can inform CPW on how moose habitat may change in the future.
